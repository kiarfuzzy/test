Choosing the Right Infill Pattern and Percentage

Infill is the easiest setting to leave on the default and the easiest one to get wrong. Here's what it actually changes, and how to pick a sensible value instead of guessing.

6 min read

Infill is the internal lattice that fills the inside of a print. It's usually described as a percentage — 100% would be a fully solid part, 0% would be hollow — but the pattern you choose matters almost as much as the number.

What infill percentage actually changes

Percentage mostly trades off four things at once: strength, weight, material cost, and print time. Going from 10% to 50% infill roughly doubles or triples how much filament the interior of a part uses, which shows up directly in cost and print time — but the strength gain isn't linear. Past a certain point, more infill adds weight and print time faster than it adds usable strength, because the outer walls and top/bottom layers are doing most of the structural work for typical loads.

If you want to see the material and cost impact of a specific percentage before you commit to it, the Material Usage Estimator will translate infill percentage into an estimated weight for your model's volume.

The main infill patterns

Most slicers offer somewhere between six and fifteen pattern options. In practice, a handful cover nearly every situation:

  • Grid — a simple crossing line pattern. Fast to print, reasonably strong in the plane of the layers, and the default in a lot of slicers for good reason.
  • Gyroid — a smooth, wavy 3D pattern that's strong in every direction rather than just along two axes. Slightly slower than grid but a good general-purpose choice, especially for parts that get stressed from more than one direction.
  • Honeycomb — strong for its weight and visually satisfying if the part is ever seen in cross-section, but noticeably slower to print than grid or gyroid at the same percentage.
  • Triangles / Tri-hexagon — very rigid, resists shearing well, and is a common pick for parts under mechanical load, like brackets or jigs.
  • Cubic — a 3D pattern that prints reasonably fast and gives fairly even strength from multiple angles, similar in spirit to gyroid but with a more angular structure.
  • Lightning — a newer, sparse pattern designed specifically to support the top surface with the least material possible. Excellent for purely decorative prints where you just need the top layers to not sag.
Top and bottom layers are separate from infill. Solid layer count (usually 3–5 layers) does more for a part's stiffness and surface finish than infill percentage does. If a print feels flimsy, try adding a couple of top/bottom layers before reaching straight for a higher infill percentage.

Picking a percentage by use case

  • Decorative or display prints: 5–10%. The part just needs to hold its shape and support the top surface.
  • Standard functional prints (brackets, organizers, enclosures): 15–25%. This is where most slicers default, and it's a reasonable middle ground.
  • Parts under real mechanical load (gears, load-bearing brackets, jigs): 30–50%, usually paired with a stronger pattern like triangles or cubic and a higher wall count.
  • Near-solid parts (small parts where strength matters more than time or material, or anything that will be drilled or tapped): 60–100%. Above roughly 80%, most slicers behave similarly to a fully solid print in terms of time and material.

A simple way to decide

Start from the use case, not the number. Pick a pattern based on how the part will be loaded — grid or gyroid for general use, triangles or cubic if it needs to resist bending or shear — and then pick the lowest percentage in the relevant range above. It's much cheaper to reprint at a higher percentage after a part fails than to over-build every print "just in case."
